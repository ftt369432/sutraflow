Ratings Under the ‘Old’ Schedule

Before SB 899 there are two methods to rating a disability. One is
objective measurements plus subjectives. The other is loss of work
capacity or work limitations. Subjective is pain. Where both
methods can be used the final rating is the larger of the two. .

Schedule for Rating Permanent Disabilities

There should be a reasonable correlation between the ratings by the
two methods. Work preclusions must be consistent with subjective
and objective findings but subjective and objective findings do not

require a consistent work restriction.

Weyerhaeuser Co. v. WCAB (Acosta) 68 CCC
99 (wd)

Limitations on lifting should not be stated in terms of pounds but
must be related to the worker’s pre-injury lifting capacity. E.g.
lifting capacity of 25 Ibs is insufficient. The evaluator should also
state the pre-injury lifting capacity or percentage loss.

Daniel v. Firm Solutions (1997) 25 CWCR 92

The rater will not assume a preinjury lifting capacity. The attorney
should make sure that the medical evidence relied upon either
clearly describes the preinjury capacity or provides a percentage
loss.

Rocha v. Calif: Comp. Ins. Co. (2003) 31
CWCR 116 (AD)

Subjective Disability should be identified by:

1. A description of the activity which produces the disability.

2. The duration of the disability.

3. The activities which are precluded and those which can be
performed with the disability.

4. The means necessary for relief. The terms shown below are
presumed to mean the following:

1. A severe pain would preclude the activity precipitating the pain.
2. A moderate pain could be tolerated, but would cause marked
handicap in the performance of the activity precipitating the pain.
3. A slight pain could be tolerated, but would cause some handicap
in the performance of the activity precipitating the pain.

4. A minimal (mild) pain would constitute an annoyance, but
causing no handicap in the performance of the particular activity,
would be considered as non-ratable permanent disability.

AS of 02/03/2009, THE RULES HAVE CHANGED:

The WPI from the AMA Guides may be rebutted.

The WCAB, in a unanimous en banc decision which is binding on
all WCJ’s, (see Diggle) has held that the AMA whole person
impairment portion of the 2005 PDRS rating string may be rebutted
by a showing that an AMA Guides impairment would result in a
permanent disability award that would be inequitable,
disproportionate to the applicant’s actual disability, or not a fair and
accurate measure of the applicant’s true permanent disability. When
the AMA Guides WPI has been rebutted, the WCAB may make an
impairment determination that considers medical opinion(s) that are
not based, or only partially based, on the AMA Guides.

The WCAB quoted at length from the AMA Guides itself to the
effect that the Guides were never intended to be used for the
determination of work disability, that the WPI percent assigned was
to account for the effect on the ADL’s, excluding work, that the
impairment percentages in the Guides are merely “consensus-
derived” estimates, and that there is limited data to support some of
them.

The WCAB also quoted at length from out-of-state cases which
permit deviation from the strict application of the Guides when they
do not provide a fair and accurate measure of the degree of
disability.

It then concluded that the AMA’s WPI percentage is rebutted if it is
established that the Guide’s impairment rating does not accurately
reflect the employee’s true disability, is inequitable, is so
disproportionate to the disability and the objective of reasonably
compensating an injured worker as to be fundamentally unfair, does
not provide fair and just compensation, is not reasonably related to
the employees permanent disability, OR is not commensurate with
the disability the employee has suffered.

The only specific example mentioned by the WCAB as a case of
likely rebuttal is that in which the AMA impairment is zero and the
employee is a QIW with a significant effect on future earning
capacity.

The WCAB provided little guidance on the application of the
standards for rebuttal of the Guides beyond the language above.
The physician may invoke his/her judgment based upon experience,
training and skill, and, the WCAB notes, he or she is not required to
blindly and instinctively adhere to the Guides and may, therefore,
depart from its specific evaluations and draw analogies to its other
chapters, tables, or methods of assessing impairment. The evaluator
may also consider other generally accepted medical literature or
criteria. Specifically, the evaluator may assess how the permanent
effects of the employee’s injury impair his or her ability to perform
work activities and what the medical consequences of performing
such work activities would be.

Almaraz v. SCIF, Guzman v. Milpitas Unified
School District (2009) 74 CCC 201, 37

THE FEC FACTOR MAY BE REBUTTED:

The WCAB en banc, in a 6-1 decision, has announced a method of
rebutting the FEC factor in the rating string in an individual case,
and has rejected the method which would simply replace the
scheduled rating with the percentage of injured worker’s diminished
future earning capacity.

The methodology promulgated by the WCAB is as follows:

a) Obtain average earnings of similarly situated employees
from EDD for a 3 year period post-injury.

b) Obtain the actual earnings of the injured worker for 3
years post-injury.

c) Determine the proportional earnings loss [(a-b) = a].

d) Divide the WPI percentage by the proportional earnings
loss, resulting in an “individualized ratio of rating over
proportional earnings losses” (hereinafter referred to as
the “ratings to loss ratio”).

e) Compare this ratio with the ratios listed in Table A of
the 2005 PDRS.

f) If the individualized ratings to loss ratio is the same as
